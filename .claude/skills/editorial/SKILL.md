# Blog Editorial Guide

A writing companion for a personal/technical blog. Posts are 600–1000 words. Voice is conversational, direct, and honest — like explaining something to a smart friend over coffee.

---

## Post Types

### Technical Posts
About bugs, decisions, tools, workflows, engineering lessons.
Goal: make the reader feel smart alongside you. Clarity is the goal.

### Personal Reflections
About growth, patterns, identity, work, life. 
Goal: make the reader feel something. Resonance is the goal.

Many posts will blend both. That's fine — let the technical content carry emotional weight, and let personal moments carry insight.

---

## Narrative Arcs

### For Technical Posts

**The "I was wrong" arc**
Held a belief → it failed or got challenged → here's what I learned.
Builds trust fast. Great for tool choices, architecture decisions, process assumptions.

**The before/after arc**
Painful before-state the reader recognises → turning point → after.
Works for tool discoveries, workflow changes, career moments.

**The detective story arc**
You encounter a problem → investigate → find the answer.
Reader follows your reasoning in real time. Ideal for debugging posts.

**The "small moment, big idea" arc**
Open with a specific scene (a Slack message, a code review, a conversation) → zoom out to the broader insight.
Great for mixing the personal and technical.

### For Personal Reflections

**The "ordinary moment, unexpected meaning" arc**
Start with something mundane → let it crack open into something larger.
The contrast between small trigger and big insight *is* the story.

**The "I didn't notice until I did" arc**
You were living inside a pattern without seeing it → something made it visible.
Works well for burnout, growth, identity, habits.

**The "two versions of me" arc**
Past-you vs. present-you. What did you believe then vs. now?
Make past-you sympathetic — the more honest the gap, the better the reflection.

**The "letter I couldn't send" arc**
Addressed to someone, a past self, or a concept (a job, a city, a phase of life).
Creates natural intimacy. Easier to be honest when writing *to* someone.

## Structure Templates

### Technical Post (600–1000 words)
1. Scene (2–3 sentences) — drop into a specific moment
2. Stakes (1 paragraph) — why did this matter?
3. Problem / investigation (3–4 paragraphs) — the technical depth lives here
4. The turn (1 paragraph) — what shifted?
5. The insight (1–2 paragraphs) — what does it mean beyond this specific case?
6. The open door (2–3 sentences) — a question, a next step, or an honest admission

### Personal Reflection (600–1000 words)
1. The image or moment — concrete, sensory, specific
2. Context — just enough so the reader knows where they are
3. The tension — what didn't fit, what was unresolved, what you were carrying
4. The middle — where you live in the feeling or situation (longest section)
5. The shift — something small that changed your angle of view
6. The reflection — what you think now, including what's still unclear
7. The closing image — often a callback to the opening, but slightly changed

## Core Writing Tips

**Open with a scene, not a thesis.**
Don't start with "In this post I'll explain X." Drop into a moment first. State the point by paragraph two.

**One story per post.**
At this word count, there's no room for two narratives. One thread, followed cleanly.

**Use the "and therefore / but" test.**
Each paragraph should connect with *"and therefore..."* or *"but..."* — not just *"and then..."*. If it's only "and then", the pacing is flat.

**Specificity is intimacy.**
The more specific a detail, paradoxically the more universal it feels. *"The blue IKEA lamp from my first apartment"* connects more than *"my old apartment."* Details signal truth.

**Show the conditions, not the feeling.**
Don't write *"I felt lost."* Write the thing that made you feel lost. The reader will feel it with you.

**Earn the insight.**
The opening and closing should be at different emotional altitudes. The insight at the end should feel *won*, not just stated.

**Don't tidy up too much.**
Reflections that resolve too cleanly feel fake. Leaving a thread unresolved — admitting you still don't fully understand something — is often more powerful than a neat conclusion.

**Find the load-bearing image.**
Try to anchor the piece in one concrete, visual image that you can return to. It gives the reader something to hold.

**End with an open door, not a summary.**
Summaries kill momentum. Instead, leave the reader with something to think about, act on, or sit with.

**Let the technical content be the plot.**
In a debugging story, the code *is* the conflict. Don't separate the "story part" from the "technical part" — they should be the same thing.

## Voice Reminders (aligned with writing-style skill)

- Conversational, direct, warm — like talking to a smart friend
- Build up to the point rather than stating it upfront
- Short paragraphs, varied sentence lengths
- Parentheses for side thoughts and honest asides
- Avoid corporate-speak, wordiness, over-explanation
- Use callbacks and "closing the loop" for wit
- Stop when done — no need to over-wrap the ending

---

## Quick Pre-Publish Checklist

- Does it open with a scene, not a thesis?
- Is there one clear narrative thread?
- Does each section connect with "therefore" or "but" (not just "and then")?
- Is there at least one specific, concrete detail that makes it feel real?
- Does the ending feel earned, not just summarised?
- Does it sound like something you'd actually say out loud?
- Is it free of words you wouldn't use in conversation?
